2  Methods of Estimation

All of STAT 262, and last week, we were concerned with concepts of probability and random variables. We used them to build models of nondeterministic (i.e., random) phenomena. We focused on distributions of random variables, including function of random variables such as the sample mean. These distributions were sometimes used to determine the likelihood of a particular event or the expected value/variance of the random variable. In each of these instances, we assumed that we knew the correct probability model. Most often, however, we are interested in a numeric characteristic of a random phenomenon that cannot be computed/observed directly. For example, we might be interested in knowing

  • the percent of people in Nebraska who buy organic food
  • the effect of a teacher professional development program on student achievement
  • the average change in blood sugar with a new diabetes drug
  • the relationship between time spent playing video games and aggressive behavior
  • the relationship between self-selected seating in a classroom and class performance

As we saw in classes like STAT 102 and STAT 212 (and STAT 414!), we typically do not know the true population parameter(s), like mean lifelength of patients on a particular treatment. Instead, we observe one or more random variables whose distributions depend on the parameter of interest. Then, we use statistical inference methods to analyze the observed values to gain knowledge about the unknown characteristic and/or make decisions based on the unknown characteristic. The rest of the semester we will be focusing on developing these statistical inference methods. In earlier classes we used these methods (under some very specific assumptions), in STAT 301 we see where these methods come from.

TipDefinition: Statistical inference

Statistical Inference refers generally to the methods by which one makes inferences or generalizations about a population and its parameters based on statistics calculated from sample data. These methods can be classified broadly into three forms:

  • Point estimation is the process by which an estimator of an unknown parameter, or function of parameters, is chosen and evaluated.

  • Interval estimation is the process by which an observed value of a statistic and the statistic’s probability distribution are used to create a range of plausible values of an unknown parameter, or function of parameters.

  • Hypothesis testing is the process by which an observed value of a statistic and the statistic’s probability distribution are used to evaluate the plausibility of a statement made about the unknown value of a parameter.

2.1 Point Estimation: Introduction

The basic situation is point estimation is as follows:

  • We want to estimate an unknown population parameter \(\theta\) (e.g., \(\mu\) or \(\lambda\); possibly vector-valued) or some function of parameters.

  • We find an estimator, i.e., a function of the random variables \(X_1,\dots, X_n\): \(T=W(X_1,\dots, X_n)\) (e.g., \(\bar X\) or \(S^2\)).

  • We observe random variables \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) (not necessarily iid), where the distribution of \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) depends on the unknown parameter \(\theta\).

  • We calculate an estimate, i.e., a function of the realized (observed) values \(x_1,\dots, x_n\): \(t=W(x_1,\dots, x_n)\) (e.g., \(\bar x\) or \(s^2\)).

Notation: \(\hat \theta\) or \(\tilde \theta\) is often used in place of \(T\) to distinguish between the unknown parameter and its estimator.

2.2 Method of Moments Estimation (MME)

The method of moments is one of the oldest methods for deriving point estimators and is due to Karl Pearson, who founded the first university statistics department at University College London. Pearson died in 1936, and during his lifetime he made many contributions to statistics, including \(R^2\) and the \(\chi^2\) test. He was the protégé of Sir Francis Galton, cousin of Charles Darwin and the inventor of the correlation coefficient, \(R\). In general, Pearson’s method of moments generates possibly good estimators, though they can often be improved. The basic idea is that sample moments are good estimators of population moments when \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) are iid. That is, \(\bar X\) is a good estimator of E\((X)\).

Common Misconceptions about MMEs:

  • Transformation of Data:

    The method of moments estimator (MME) is NOT invariant to transformation of the data. For example, if \(X_1,\dots, X_n \overset{iid}{\sim}\) N\((\mu,\sigma^2)\), but we observe \(Y_1,\dots, Y_n\) where \(Y_i = e^{X_i}\), \(i=1,\dots, n\), then a MME of \(\underline{\theta} = \left (\begin{array}{c} \mu \\ \sigma^2 \end{array}\right)\) is not the same as the estimator we found earlier based on \(X_1,\dots, X_n\).

  • Matching the First Moments:

    The first sample and population moments are NOT always the moments that will be matched, even if the distribution has only one unknown parameter. For example, the Laplace distribution, with pdf \[f_X(x;\beta) = \frac{1}{2\beta} e^{-|x|/\beta} \hspace{10mm} -\infty < x < \infty, \hspace{3mm} \beta > 0,\] has E\((X) = 0\). So, if \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) is a random sample from the Laplace distribution, setting the first population moment, \(\mu_1=0\), equal to the first sample moment, \(M_1=\bar X\), does not yield an estimate of the unknown parameter \(\beta\). However, \(\mu_2 = \hbox{E}(X^2) = 2\beta^2\), so we can use the second sample moment to get a MME.

2.3 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Maximum likelihood estimation is another method used to find point estimators. It was named and popularized by Sir R. A. Fisher in the early 1920s. The basic idea is that, given the sample data, choose as the estimator (e.g., \(\hat N\)) of the parameter (e.g., \(N\)) the value that makes the observed sample result the most “likely,” i.e., maximize the “likelihood.”

Advantage of MLEs: Invariance!

Often, we know the MLE of an unknown parameter, but we’re really interested in estimating a function of the unknown parameter. MLEs are invariant to transformations of parameter.

TipTheorem: Invariance Property of MLEs

If \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE of \(\theta\), then for any function \(\tau(\theta)\), the MLE of \(\tau(\theta)\) is \(\tau(\hat\theta)\).

Other considerations when calculating MLEs:

  • Numerical optimization:

    For many problems we’ll see in STAT 301, we use calculus to find \(\hat\theta_{\hbox{\tiny{MLE}}}\) as an explicit function of the data, e.g., \(\hat\theta_{\hbox{\tiny{MLE}}} = \bar X\). Most real life problems, however, require numerical optimization, which essentially involves searching the parameter space \(\Theta\) for a \(\hat\theta_{\hbox{\tiny{MLE}}}\) that maximizes the likelihood function for all \(\theta \in \Theta\).

  • Numerical instability:

    Because MLEs are found using a maximization process, they are susceptible to problems associated with that process, including numerical instability. Problems occur when the likelihood function is very flat in the neighborhood of the maximum or when there is no finite maximum.

Bayesian analysis and estimation are based on a fundamentally different philosphy than likelihood/frequentist based methods. Why use Bayesian approaches?

  • Allow the user to account for uncertainty in the systems we’re trying to model, and more realistically capture the data-generating process

  • Allow the user to build custom models, rather than try to shove a complex problem in an existing box

  • More intuitive interpretations for some quantities (we’ll see this with interval estimates)

  • Some models can only be fit using Bayesian methods

2.5 What’s Next?

We have seen several methods of obtaining point estimators. This leads to question: when faced with the choice of two or more estimators for the same parameter, which do we choose? Which estimator is “better”? There is not a universally appropriate measure of “goodness,” but we can define some properties that will help us decide whether one (frequentist) estimator is better than another. In this context, we’ll be thinking of \(\theta\) as a scale parameter.
