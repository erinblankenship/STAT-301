Final Project

Throughout this semester, we’ve been starting with a sample of random variables \(X_1,\dots, X_n\) drawn from a population with a known distribution but unknown values for the parameters. We’ve focused on estimating those unknown parameters, based on the sample drawn. This is actually pretty unrealistic. Typically, we are presented with a set of data and tasked with answering a research question. For example, consider the following data set. Students were asked how much time (in hours) they spent playing video games in the prior week, and we’d like to make a general statement about how much time all US teenagers spend playing games based on these sample data (taken from Nolan and Speed, 2000).

Because time playing video games is a continuous, nonnegative random variable, it would seem that an exponential distribution would be a reasonable model for these data. However, the recorded time was grouped into categories, and is therefore a discrete random variable. In addition, over half of the values are zero. Therefore, assuming that recorded time follows an exponential distribution is not reasonable, and we cannot use the parameter from the exponential distribution to characterize the underlying population.

If we want to construct an estimator to answer our research question, we cannot use the method of moments or maximum likelihood because we don’t have a pdf. We might think the sample mean would still be a good estimator, but we can’t declare it a UMVUE, again because we don’t have a pdf to start from. We can, however, take a different approach to examine the behavior, or “goodness” of an estimator. One method we could use is the bootstrap, which you used a lot in STAT 102. As a reminder, the idea behind the bootstrap is pretty simple. We don’t know the distribution of the underlying population, which means we don’t know the distribution of any candidate statistics we’d like to use. So, for example, because we don’t know the underlying distribution of video game times, we don’t know the distribution of statistics like \(\bar{X}\) or the sample median. However, we could estimate the distribution of statistics just like we estimate parameters using the data set we observed. The general procedure for the bootstrap is as follows:

  1. Create many data sets, each the same size as the original data set, called resamples. These resamples are collected by sampling with replacement from the data.

  2. Calculate the statistic of interest for each resample. The distribution of the resample statistics is called a bootstrap distribution, and is an estimate of the true underlying sampling distribution of the statistic.

This resulting bootstrap distribution could be used just as we would use any other sampling distribution–to assess bias, the variability of the statistic, etc.

From the bootstrap distribution of the sample mean, we see that estimated sampling distribution is skewed to the right and that a typical estimate falls within about 0.25 hours (15 minutes) of the center of the distribution. We can quantify these conclusions by looking at the three quartiles of the bootstrap distribution (Q1=0.96 hours, median=1.21 hours, and Q3=1.49 hours). Not surprisingly, the estimated bias of the sample mean (1.247-1.243=0.004 hours) is very close to zero.
